Lab 2 — Step 1: Research

Audience Persona

Narrative profile of the primary TASS user.

Dr. Maya Chen is a 34-year-old assistant professor of communication studies at a mid-sized public university in the Midwest. She earned her PhD from the University of Wisconsin-Madison in 2022, where her dissertation used computational text analysis to study framing in local news coverage of police reform. She is now in her third year on the tenure track, teaching two courses per semester while managing a research agenda that depends on publishing at least two journal articles per year to stay on pace for her tenure review in 2028.

Maya's research examines how news organizations frame social justice issues across digital platforms. Her method involves collecting large text corpora — tweets, news articles, public comments — and scoring them against sentiment and moral foundations dictionaries. During her doctoral work, she used LIWC-22 through a university site license and supplemented it with custom R scripts her advisor helped her write. She has not written R code independently since graduating.

This is the core of Maya's frustration. Her current institution does not have a LIWC site license, and the individual subscription costs $200 per year — a line item her startup fund technically covers but that requires a purchase justification memo every renewal. More importantly, the R scripts she inherited are brittle. They break when she updates packages, they produce output she has to manually reformat for APA tables, and they take her an entire afternoon to debug when something goes wrong. She spends more time troubleshooting her tools than analyzing her data.

Maya values methodological rigor above speed. She would rather spend an extra day verifying an effect size than rush a finding into a manuscript. She fears being perceived as a "methods tourist" — someone who uses computational approaches without truly understanding them — and she compensates by over-documenting her analytical decisions. Her biggest professional aspiration is to publish a single-authored article in Political Communication before her tenure review. Her biggest daily frustration is the gap between the research she wants to do and the tools she can actually operate without help.

She consumes academic content primarily through Google Scholar alerts, Twitter/X (which she uses passively — reading threads from methods scholars but rarely posting), and discipline-specific email listservs like CRTNET. She subscribes to The Chronicle of Higher Education and reads Inside Higher Ed headlines in her email but rarely clicks through. She does not use Instagram for professional purposes. On LinkedIn, she maintains a profile but posts only when a new publication comes out. She spends roughly 45 minutes per day on academic social media, almost entirely on her phone during her commute.

The Hook: Maya discovers TASS through a tweet from a methods scholar she follows, downloads it in under five minutes, and runs her first dictionary analysis on a corpus she has been sitting on for three months — without writing a single line of code, without creating a cloud account, and without asking her department's IT office for permission.
