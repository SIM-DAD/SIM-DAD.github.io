Campaign Case Study

The TASS launch analyzed through the 9-Step Strategic Planning Process.

Presenter: Alex Leith, PhD

Case: SIM DAD LLC — TASS v1.0 Product Launch Campaign

Duration: 5 minutes + 2-minute Q&A

Visual aid: Single slide showing the campaign timeline and KPI results framework

Teaching note: This example treats a planned campaign as a case study, which is a departure from analyzing someone else's historical case. The analytical structure — Situation, Analysis, Verdict, Lesson — is identical. Students should note how the 9-Step framework applies to a real, small-scale campaign rather than a Fortune 500 crisis.

1. The Situation (30-45 seconds)

SIM DAD LLC is a one-person software company that built TASS, a no-code text analysis tool for researchers. The communication challenge: how does a solo founder with no marketing budget, no PR agency and no existing audience launch a niche desktop application into a market dominated by a single entrenched competitor (LIWC-22) that has 20 years of institutional adoption?

The campaign had to accomplish three things simultaneously: make researchers aware the product exists, convince them it is methodologically credible and convert awareness into paid subscriptions — all without a sales team, a conference booth or paid advertising.

2. The Analysis (2-2.5 minutes)

Step 1 — Research: The campaign began with audience research (Lab 2). The target persona — a tenure-track social scientist who needs text analysis but cannot code — was identified through direct observation in the classroom. This was not hypothetical market research. The founder teaches the methods course and has watched this exact user struggle for a decade. The research phase also included competitive analysis: LIWC-22's pricing ($200/year per seat, no institutional volume discount below enterprise tier), its cloud-only architecture and its lack of built-in statistical testing.

Step 3 — Organizing: The Strategic Message Planner (Lab 4) anchored the campaign on three key messages, with "Your data deserves better tools" as the primary positioning statement. The tactical plan mapped each campaign deliverable to a specific launch phase: backgrounder and blog post pre-launch, news release and media pitch at launch, evaluation plan post-launch. This was not a scatter-shot approach — each piece served a defined role in a sequenced timeline.

Step 4 — Writing: Four distinct voices were deployed across the campaign. The news release (Lab 5) used journalist voice — inverted pyramid, AP Style, attributed quotes. The executive blog post (Lab 7) used first-person thought leadership to establish credibility. The internal memo (Lab 8) used corporate voice to communicate a real business disruption (payment platform rejection). The print ad (Lab 6) used persuasive voice with AIDA structure targeting the identified persona. Each voice was chosen for its audience, not as a stylistic exercise.

Step 8 — Distributing: The media pitch (Lab 9) targeted a specific journalist at Inside Higher Ed who covers faculty technology adoption — not a generic press list. The social media calendar (Lab 3) deployed a 30-day content sequence across LinkedIn and Instagram, timed to build awareness before launch day. Distribution was deliberate and narrow: reach the people who would actually buy the product, not maximize impressions.

3. The Verdict (~1 minute)

The campaign's success is defined by four KPIs established in the evaluation plan (Lab 10): 200 paid subscriptions in 60 days, 10 earned media mentions in 90 days, a 10% trial activation rate from the product page and 3%+ social media engagement rate.

As of this presentation, the campaign is pre-launch — the verdict is prospective. The framework, however, is falsifiable. If the campaign fails to hit 200 subscriptions, the diagnosis will be found in which KPI underperformed: low media mentions suggest a distribution failure (Step 8), low trial activation suggests a messaging failure (Step 4) and low engagement suggests an audience failure (Step 1). The evaluation plan does not just measure success — it diagnoses the specific point of failure.

4. The Lesson (45-60 seconds)

The transferable lesson is this: a campaign does not need a budget to be strategic. It needs a framework. The 9-Step Process gave this campaign structure that a marketing budget could not buy: research that was grounded in direct observation, messaging that was tested against a real persona, distribution that was targeted at a named journalist rather than a press list and evaluation criteria that diagnose failure rather than just measuring it.

For your own portfolio work: if you can name the person you are trying to reach, explain why your message matters to them specifically and define what success looks like before you launch, you have a strategy. Everything else is execution.

Discussion question for Q&A: The founder is also the product, the marketer and the CEO. How does that constraint — no separation between creator and communicator — change the ethical obligations of the campaign? Is an "objective" news release possible when the person quoted is also the person who wrote it?
